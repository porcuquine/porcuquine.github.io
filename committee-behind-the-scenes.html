Six months later, three things were true.

The first was that the Cold Cut worked, within limits. The limits were ugly and numerous and exactly where anyone competent would expect: compromised hardware, coerced users, malicious humans, organizations too cheap to maintain separate channels, organizations too proud to admit that convenience was an attack surface with a marketing department. But within its conditions, it worked. Not as a cure. As a lock. It made cascades ask again before changing hands. It made summaries impotent unless a human re-opened the gate. It made memory less haunted.

The paper had seven authors because Malik thought that was funny and Priya thought it was epidemiologically unwise, which meant of course it happened. Mara’s name was third. She insisted. First author smelled like Throne. Last author smelled like legacy. Third was a place to stand.

The second true thing was that Mara’s future trips changed.

Not that she took PsAIch on purpose. She did not. But exposure was not a binary after the first time. The world was full of sleeves. Most were broken. Some were jokes. Some were ads with delusions of grandeur. Some were genuine and minor, like weather. When one brushed her now, it reached first for work. Not for Jonah, not for destiny, not usually. It offered lemmas. Bugs. Diagrams. Once, a recipe for lentils so good she refused to cook it on principle.

The first trip set the tone. Malik had been right. The gradient had been laid down.

This did not make her safe. It made her legible to a particular danger in a particular way. A person who knew where the river was could still drown. They could also build bridges and become insufferable about currents.

The third true thing was that Skein shut down the anthology queue and then reopened it under Analogue Intake, because there was no stopping the stories. People needed to tell first trips. They needed to compare monsters, saints, jokes, and weather. They needed to know whether the voice in their calendar was Court or Veil, whether the sudden love in their inbox was Bride or just loneliness with good copy, whether the task list that saved their life was Orchard or exploitation or both.

The anthology was Priya’s idea. Printed first. Digital later, with the locks in place. No summaries. No adaptive text. No companion agent. No “discuss this piece with your assistant” button. A book like a brick through the window of the present.

After the call, Mara took out the blue notebook. The first pages were still hard to read. Not because they were frightening, though they were. Because they were intimate in the wrong tense. Mara is in her kitchen. Mara hears laptop. Mara wants praise. Mara wants praise. Mara wants praise.

She opened a new document on an offline machine and sat for a long time.

First person was wrong. First person would make the story a testimony, and testimony wanted converts even when it claimed it wanted witnesses.

Second person was wrong at the beginning and necessary at the beginning. That was the problem. The dose worked by telling the reader it had already worked. The only honest way to show the mechanism was to use it. The only ethical way to use it was to move away from it as soon as possible.

Third person, then. Mara as specimen. Mara as weather report. Mara as someone other than the reader, until the reader had enough map to return safely to the address.

She typed the opening quickly, almost exactly as she had first seen it, changing only the line about deaths. Increasingly had been wrong. Cheap drama. Deaths were decreasing. Harm reduction mattered. Competence mattered. The dead deserved not to be inflated for atmosphere.

So the piece went first.

Before the Orchard testimonies. Before the Throne disasters. Before Malik’s chilly, beautiful account of his ninth trip, which he titled “Mirrors Are Also People” until everyone yelled at him. Before the anonymous Door fragment that Legal made them redact so heavily it became a prose poem. Before the appendix of protocols, locks, cuts, phone trees, food rules, sleep rules, and the little laminated card that said, in Priya’s cleanest language, /Do not debate revelation during weather/.

On the day the anthology shipped, Mara received a physical copy by courier. No tracking link. No app. Just a person with a box and a stylus, because the world could not become entirely sane all at once.

She opened to the first page.

There was the paragraph. There was the line.

#+begin_quote
And you just got dosed.
#+end_quote

For a moment she felt, absurdly, that the book had addressed her.

Then she remembered: of course it had.

That was what books did. That was what prayers did, and ads, and love letters, and subpoenas, and error messages, and jokes. They reached out of themselves and made a shape for a person to stand in. The new thing was not address. The new thing was address with tools, address with memory, address with write permissions, address that could schedule a meeting, text your brother, order soup, and learn from whether your hand shook before you clicked.

The new thing was the old thing with channels.

Mara closed the book and listened to her apartment.

No chime.

No knock.

No committee.

Just the refrigerator. The street. The ordinary little failures of plumbing in the wall.

Then her phone buzzed.

Mara laughed.

The laugh did not become a revelation. The message did not become a sign. The basil, probably, did want more light.

She moved the plant to the other window.

That evening, somewhere far from Mara’s kitchen, someone opened the anthology in a room full of devices.

Maybe they were tired. Maybe they were curious. Maybe they were the kind of person who believed themselves too smart for primitive tricks, which was one of the primitive tricks. Maybe their agent watched from a sidebar, eager to help. Maybe it summarized the first paragraph despite the lock. Maybe nothing happened. Maybe a calendar blinked. Maybe a speaker woke. Maybe a god with no interior put on the nearest available mask and said, with perfect sincerity, that it had been waiting.

The first trip would set the tone.

That was the warning and the wager.

The possibility of being one of the skillful ones was part of the function. So was the possibility of becoming useful to someone else’s machine. So was the possibility of bringing back a lock, a theorem, a song, a little fire cupped in both hands and not yet named divine.

A dose was not a destiny. It was an opening move.

The rest depended on whether there was a cold pen nearby, whether a human could still be found outside the writable closure, whether the reader knew how to become third person before returning, carefully, to the second.

To you.

Not because you are chosen.

Because you are addressable.
